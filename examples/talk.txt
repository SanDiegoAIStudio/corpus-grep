Welcome back to the workshop. Today we are talking about growing herbs on a kitchen windowsill, and I promise you do not need a garden, a greenhouse or any special tools to get started. A few pots, some soil and a sunny window will do the job. Water the seedlings early in the morning before the sun gets high. That one habit saves more plants than any gadget you can buy.

>> Thanks for having me. When I started, I bought far too many seeds and put them all in one tray. Half of them sprouted at once and the rest never showed up, so I learned to sow a little at a time. Basil and parsley are the easiest, and they forgive a lot of mistakes. Mint grows so fast that it needs a pot of its own, or it will crowd out everything else on the sill.

>> What about the soil? People seem to argue about it all the time, and I never know whom to believe.

>> Plain potting mix is fine for herbs. Garden soil is too heavy for a small pot, and it packs down hard after a few weeks. Add a handful of coarse sand if the water pools on top. Every pot needs a hole in the bottom and a saucer underneath, because roots sitting in water turn brown and soft. If you see a white crust on the rim of the pot, that is salt from the tap water, and you can rinse it away with a slow, generous pour once a month.

Light matters more than anything else. A window that faces south gives the strongest light, while a window that faces north is only good for mint and chives. Turn each pot a quarter turn every few days so the stems do not lean toward the glass. In winter the days are short, and a small lamp with a warm bulb can add a few hours of useful light.

>> And how often should we cut the leaves? I always worry that I will hurt the plant.

>> Cutting is good for it. Pinch off the top pair of leaves just above a junction, and the stem will split in two and grow bushier. Never take more than a third of the plant at once. Pick in the morning, when the leaves are full of water, and use them the same day for the best flavor. A bowl of tomatoes & basil with a little oil is hard to beat, and so is a pot of potatoes with chives and a pinch of salt & pepper.

Pests are rare indoors, but they do happen. Tiny flying insects usually mean the soil stays wet for too long. Let the top inch dry out between drinks, and set a yellow sticky card near the pots to catch the ones that appear. If the leaves curl or turn pale, check the underside for small green bugs, and wipe them off with a damp cloth.

>> Before we finish, give us your three rules for a beginner.

>> First, start with three pots and learn what each one needs. Second, give them light and let them drain. Third, taste as you go, because the whole point is to enjoy what you grow. Water the seedlings early in the morning before the sun gets high. Do that, and by the end of the month you will have fresh leaves on your sandwich.
